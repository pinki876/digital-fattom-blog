Why Spain Was the World Cup 2026 Favorite — And Why That Almost Never Happens

Spain just won the 2026 World Cup as the team everyone predicted would win it. That sounds normal. It isn't. Here's the full story: the security fears before a ball was even kicked, who the favorites actually were, and why "favorite" teams almost always choke before finals even start.

The tournament everyone was watching, for more reasons than football

The 2026 World Cup was always going to be the biggest ever — 48 teams instead of 32, 104 matches, three host countries (USA, Mexico, Canada), and 16 stadiums. But long before kickoff on June 11, the bigger story wasn't the football. It was security.

This was the first World Cup ever hosted partly by a country actively at war with a participating nation, with tensions from the US-Iran conflict spilling directly into tournament planning. Every US match was designated a National Special Security Event — the same classification used for presidential inaugurations. Officials were tracking threats ranging from drone attacks to homegrown extremism, and just weeks before the opening match, hundreds of millions of dollars in promised federal security funding still hadn't been released, forcing host cities to plan without knowing their full budget.

What people were actually scared of: not a single dramatic attack, but a mix of smaller risks stacking up — drone incursions near stadiums, cyberattacks on ticketing systems, overwhelmed transport hubs, and copycat "lone wolf" incidents inspired by global tensions rather than directed by them.

None of the worst fears materialized. The tournament ran, largely, without major incident — which itself became a quiet success story for the thousands of officers, agencies, and AI-powered threat monitoring systems working behind the scenes across three countries for 39 straight days.

So who was actually the favorite?

Before a ball was kicked, bookmakers had it down to two teams: Spain and France, both sitting around +450 to win it all. Spain had just come off a European Championship win, and their squad — anchored by a teenage sensation in Lamine Yamal — was widely considered the most complete side in the tournament. England, Brazil, and defending champions Argentina rounded out the next tier of contenders.

Spain wobbled early — a shock 0-0 draw against tournament debutants Cape Verde in their opener had bettors nervous. France briefly took over as the solo favorite heading into the semifinals. But Spain's semifinal performance changed everything: they shut down Kylian Mbappé and beat France 2-0, tying an all-time record for consecutive wins by a national team.

Why "favorite" teams almost never actually win

Here's the part most casual fans don't know: being the pre-tournament favorite is historically a warning sign, not a guarantee. Before this year, only three teams in World Cup history had entered as the clear favorite and actually won it — West Germany in 1974, Brazil in 1994, and Spain themselves in 2010.

Even more brutal is what's known in football circles as the "champions' curse" — the pattern of the reigning World Cup winner collapsing in their very next tournament:

That's four out of five reigning champions crashing out before the knockout rounds even began. The reasons repeat every time: aging squads that don't get refreshed, opponents who've spent four years studying exactly how the champion plays, and — harder to measure but very real — a loss of hunger once a team has already reached the mountaintop.

This same pattern hit Argentina in 2026. As defending champions chasing a historic fourth straight major trophy, Lionel Messi's side reached the final — but managed just two shots on goal across the entire 120-minute match, going out with a whimper rather than a bang.

Why Spain finally broke the pattern

So what made this Spain side different from every "sure thing" that came before it? A few things stack up:

  1. They weren't defending anything. Spain's last World Cup title was 2010 — 16 years earlier. There was no trophy to protect, no aging core to manage, no complacency to fight. They arrived hungry, not tired.
  2. Genuine squad depth, not just star power. Spain could rotate players position-for-position without losing quality — the exact trait that separates tournament winners from teams that peak too early.
  3. A finishing weapon off the bench. Substitute Ferran Torres, not a starting superstar, scored the winning goal in extra time — a sign of a squad deep enough to win games even when the first-choice XI is being shut down.
  4. Defense that didn't crack under pressure. Spain conceded just once in the entire tournament and didn't allow Argentina a single shot on target across 90 minutes of the final.
  5. A settled tactical identity. Manager Luis de la Fuente had built and stuck with one system for years, rather than experimenting under pressure — the same stability that experts flagged as a strength before the tournament even began.

The final itself told the whole story. Spain dominated possession and chances (1.94 expected goals to Argentina's 0.20) but couldn't find a way past a heroic Emiliano Martínez — who set a World Cup final record with 11 saves — until 106 minutes in, when Ferran Torres finally broke through. Spain became world champions for the second time, and the first-ever pre-tournament favorite to actually win it since 2010.

What it means going forward

For Messi, this was almost certainly his last World Cup — he's said he'll be 43 by 2030. For Spain, the bigger question is the one every champion eventually faces: can they be the team that finally breaks the curse twice, or will 2030 be their own group-stage nightmare? History says the odds aren't in their favor. But then again, until July 19, 2026, history also said the favorite wasn't supposed to win.
